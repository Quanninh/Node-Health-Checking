\begin{abstract}
Decentralized overlay networks rely on random regular topologies to guarantee properties such as low diameter and resilience to node failures. While the literature extensively analyzes macroscopic outcomes---network expansion, churn tolerance, and peer sampling quality---it seldom examines the micro-level mechanics of node integration. In particular, achieving join-specific bounded-degree randomness remains underexplored: how can a single joining node reach a fixed target degree through local edge redistribution without introducing structural bias into the overlay's indegree distribution, clustering, or load balance?

We present FINDING NEMO, a fully decentralized join-and-evict protocol for bounded-degree random overlay construction. A new node samples existing members and selects $k/2$ targets; each target accommodates the newcomer by evicting one existing neighbor, who is subsequently rewired to the joining node. This localized mechanism allows the newcomer to attain its fixed degree $k$ while preserving the network's bounded density and uniform structural randomness. We formalize why exactly $k/2$ existing edges must be broken during each join via the Handshake Lemma, prove that the protocol deterministically avoids self-loops and multi-edges, and model the concurrency boundary through a thinned Poisson arrival process. Simulation across 4{,}260 independent runs confirms 100\% convergence under sequential churn and in macro-scale overlays ($N = 10^5$), and precisely delineates the contention regime in sparse networks.

\keywords{Distributed Systems \and Peer-to-Peer \and Node Joining.}

\section{Introduction and Related Work}

Overlay networks form the communication backbone of large-scale distributed systems, providing the substrate over which peers coordinate, disseminate data, and detect failures~\cite{patelSurveyRecentAdvancements2025}. A well-established approach to overlay design is to construct a random regular graph: a topology in which every node maintains a fixed number of connections. Such topologies exhibit low diameter, high expansion, and inherent load balance, making them attractive for peer-to-peer (P2P) applications ranging from health checking~\cite{dadgarLifeguardLocalHealth2017,yangMedleyMembershipService2022} to Byzantine-resilient consensus~\cite{guptaFullyDistributedProtocolConstructing2025}.

Substantial work has addressed the global properties of random overlays---expansion maintenance under churn, gossip convergence, and spectral guarantees---yet comparatively little attention has been devoted to the precise rules by which a single node integrates into a bounded-degree topology. The literature is rich on whole-overlay outcomes but thin on micro-level join mechanics, specifically on eviction-triggered rewiring and the structural biases it can introduce. We address this gap with FINDING NEMO (Finding Neighbors and Evicting Members of their Own), a protocol that isolates the neighbor-discovery logic required to preserve $k$-regularity during node integration. The remainder of this section synthesizes the related work that motivates and contextualizes our approach.

\subsubsection{Peer Sampling and Gossip-Based Overlays.}
Gossip protocols maintain connected overlays through periodic, randomized partial-view exchanges. CYCLON~\cite{CYCLONInexpensiveMembership} is a representative example: each peer regularly shuffles a subset of neighbor descriptors with a randomly chosen partner, producing topologies with properties close to random graphs. CYCLON achieves low clustering and short diameters at minimal bandwidth; however, its degree distribution fluctuates stochastically around an average ($\sigma^2 > 0$), and it does not enforce a hard degree bound. More recently, Guerraoui et~al.~\cite{guerraouiPeerSwapPeerSamplerRandomness2024} introduced PeerSwap, the first gossip-based peer sampler with provable finite-time convergence guarantees. By mapping neighborhood swaps to an interchange process on a fixed graph, PeerSwap achieves polylogarithmic mixing time on expanders while preserving the initial topology's structural invariants. Notably, PeerSwap assumes that the underlying regular graph already exists; our work is complementary, as FINDING NEMO constructs the $k$-regular substrate on which such a sampler can subsequently operate.

\subsubsection{Expander Graph Construction and Churn Resilience.}
Sparse expander graphs have long been recognized as the structural foundation for fault-tolerant decentralized communication. Law and Siu~\cite{lawDistributedConstructionRandom2003} proposed a distributed scheme that constructs random expanders by embedding $d$ edge-disjoint Hamiltonian cycles, using $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ random walks to locate and split existing edges. While this construction achieves near-optimal spectral gaps, maintaining global Hamiltonian cycles under churn is fragile: departures break cycles and necessitate complex stitching. Poonpakdee and Di~Fatta~\cite{poonpakdeeRobustEfficientMembership2017} introduced EMP+, demonstrating that uncoordinated churn in epidemic protocols can cause permanent network partitioning through message interleaving, and employed continuous expansion monitoring to preserve connectivity. Cruciani~\cite{crucianiMaintainingBoundedDegree2025} generalized the RAES protocol of Becchetti et~al.\ to dynamic networks, proving that a threshold-based connection strategy maintains a constant-degree expander with high probability under adversarial churn of up to $\mathcal{O}(n / \text{polylog}(n))$ per round. Angileri et~al.~\cite{angileriThresholdDrivenStreamingGraph2025} extended this line of work to a streaming sliding-window model, showing that bounded-degree snapshots retain good vertex expansion and support $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ rumor spreading. Gupta and Pandurangan~\cite{guptaFullyDistributedProtocolConstructing2025} formalized the maintenance of bounded-degree expanders in the presence of full-information Byzantine adversaries, relying on carefully scheduled random walks and token-based verification to tolerate up to $o(n / \text{polylog}\,n)$ malicious nodes.

FINDING NEMO differs from all these approaches in scope and mechanism. Rather than maintaining expansion across epochs of churn, we isolate the atomic join event and show that a single-hop eviction hand-off ($\mathcal{O}(1)$ operations per target) suffices to preserve exact $k$-regularity---without rigid cycle dependencies, multi-hop walk overhead, or periodic edge refreshing.

\subsubsection{Overlay Topology Design and Message Complexity.}
Chang et~al.~\cite{changOverlayNetworkConstruction2025} studied the problem of reconfiguring an arbitrary connected graph into a bounded-degree expander or well-formed tree, achieving $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$ message complexity in the GOSSIP-reply model. Augustine et~al.~\cite{augustineHighlyDynamicFully2025} built on the expander-maintenance framework to construct and maintain distributed skip lists under heavy adversarial churn, relying on multi-overlay architectures with $\mathcal{O}(\text{polylog}\,n)$ per-node communication. Both works address global reconfiguration; FINDING NEMO targets the narrower but counterpart problem of individual node integration.

Topology shape itself is a design choice with significant consequences. Kiffer and Rajaraman~\cite{kifferStabilityP2PNetworks2024} proved that greedy peering---where nodes selectively replace connections to minimize latency to content producers---drives P2P networks toward centralized, low-diameter cliques that benefit well-connected nodes at the expense of the periphery. In contrast, Legheraba et~al.~\cite{legherabaEmergentPeertoPeerMultiHub2026} deliberately exploit preferential attachment to produce emergent multi-hub overlays with diameter~2, trading uniform degree distribution for rapid information dissemination. The self-organizing approach of Zhao et~al.~\cite{PDFResilientNetworks} takes yet another path: starting from an arbitrary connected graph, their protocol applies local edge swaps with autonomous link ownership to converge toward an approximate $k$-regular topology with spectral guarantees. FINDING NEMO occupies a distinct niche: it enforces exact $k$-regularity ($\sigma^2 = 0$ at convergence) rather than approximate regularity, and it operates at the point of node entry rather than through continuous self-reorganization.

\subsubsection{Health Checking, Failure Detection, and Membership.}
In decentralized failure detection, the SWIM protocol family decouples weakly consistent membership state from health monitoring. Lifeguard~\cite{dadgarLifeguardLocalHealth2017} introduces Local Health Awareness, dynamically scaling suspicion timeouts when local CPU or network queues degrade, thereby suppressing cascading false positives. Medley~\cite{yangMedleyMembershipService2022} adapts SWIM to IoT constraints, replacing uniform random probing with distance-skewed spatial pinging to reduce bandwidth. Li and Zs\'{o}k~\cite{liEnergyefficientLazyGroup2025} pursue energy efficiency by eliminating periodic gossip entirely, implementing an event-driven membership protocol in Haskell that emits messages only when state changes occur. While these protocols operate along the temporal and protocol dimensions---adjusting \emph{when} and \emph{how often} to probe---FINDING NEMO addresses the structural prerequisite: ensuring that every node is supervised by exactly $k$ independent peers. This invariant prevents both under-monitored nodes (delayed failure detection) and over-subscribed nodes (message bottlenecks triggering false alerts).

\subsubsection{Byzantine-Tolerant Peer Sampling.}
Adversarial settings demand sampling mechanisms that resist view poisoning and eclipse attacks. Zhang and Venkatakrishnan~\cite{zhangHoneybeeByzantineTolerant2025} proposed Honeybee, which uses verifiable random walks and table consistency checks to achieve near-uniform sampling even when over 50\% of nodes are Byzantine. Mukam et~al.~\cite{mukamAUPECollaborativeByzantine} introduced AUPE, a collaborative Byzantine-tolerant peer sampling protocol that leverages a small fraction of trusted nodes to debias views, increasing BRAHMS tolerance by up to 60\%. FINDING NEMO does not incorporate Byzantine resilience and assumes honest participants; extending the protocol with adversary-aware sampling is a natural direction for future work.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
We operate under the assumption that each node must maintain a fixed $k$ number of neighbors, where $k$ is an even integer. This constraint ensures that the overlay is neither too dense---which would harm scalability and bandwidth---nor too sparse, which would risk isolation and fragmentation. The requirement originates from our prior work on decentralized node health checking, where each node must be supervised by a bounded number of peers to avoid false-positive failure detections. The even-degree constraint is necessary for the edge-rewiring methodology described in Section~\ref{sec:proposed}.

Operationally, a newly spawned node broadcasts an initial join request to the $n$-node network, incurring $n$ messages. Responding nodes communicate with one of their existing neighbors under specific conditions to execute edge hand-offs. Because $k$ is a constant, these local rewiring operations do not grow with $n$, preserving asymptotic efficiency.

We define two foundational concepts used throughout:
(1)~\textbf{Neighbor:} a symmetric, bidirectional link between two active nodes; if $u$ lists $v$, then $v$ lists $u$.
(2)~\textbf{Neighbor List:} a local routing table of fixed capacity~$k$ maintained independently by each node. To preserve connectivity and fault resilience, each node continuously strives to keep its neighbor list fully populated whenever sufficient active peers exist.


\section{Proposed Solution}
\label{sec:proposed}
The FINDING NEMO protocol begins when a newly created node requests to join the network. To promote random neighbor selection, the joining node broadcasts a join request to all existing nodes. Given ideal connectivity, two topological cases arise.

\subsubsection{Case 1: Small Network Phase.}
If the network contains at most $k+1$ nodes, the joining node connects to all existing members. No restructuring is needed; the node reaches its target degree directly. For instance, in a four-node network with $k=4$, a fifth node simply connects to all four peers, satisfying its neighbor requirement.

\subsubsection{Case 2: Scaled Network Phase.}
If the network contains more than $k+1$ nodes, all existing nodes are assumed to have reached their target degree~$k$. These nodes still acknowledge the join request. After collecting acknowledgements, the joining node constructs a \textit{join plan} by randomly selecting $k/2$ respondents.

The joining node immediately connects to these $k/2$ targets. Each target then randomly evicts one of its current neighbors. The evicted nodes are instructed to connect to the joining node. Consequently, the newcomer acquires $k/2$ direct neighbors and $k/2$ evicted neighbors, for a total of exactly~$k$.

In practice, a hybrid selection strategy prioritizes nodes whose neighbor lists are not yet full during acknowledgement processing, accelerating the completion of underpopulated lists.

\subsection{Edge Conservation via the Handshake Lemma}
A natural question arises: why not simply connect the new node to $k$ existing peers without eviction? By the Handshake Lemma, a $k$-regular graph on $n$ nodes has exactly $nk/2$ edges. After a join, the target is $(n+1)k/2$ edges---a net increase of exactly $k/2$. If the new node created $k$ fresh edges without breaking any, the bounded-degree property would be destroyed. The protocol therefore breaks exactly $k/2$ existing connections and rewires them, which is the mathematical foundation of the eviction mechanism.

\subsection{Graph Simplicity}
Under the Configuration Model, the probability that a random pairing produces a simple graph (free of self-loops and multi-edges) decays as $\exp\left(-\frac{k^2 - 1}{4}\right)$. FINDING NEMO sidesteps this decay: during join-plan construction, an exclusion set tracks the joining node's own identifier and previously selected targets, deterministically preventing self-loops and multi-edges.

\subsection{Asymptotic Uniformity}
Preferential attachment naturally creates disproportionately dense hubs, undermining structural robustness. In the Configuration Model, unbiased random graphs are constructed by uniformly matching $nk$ half-edges. FINDING NEMO simulates this process in a decentralized environment: the joining node samples targets uniformly at random, and each target selects an eviction victim uniformly from its neighbor list, mimicking the breaking and re-pairing of a random half-edge. This mechanism resists structural bias and preserves bounded-degree uniformity.

\subsection{Space Complexity}
Each node stores a neighbor list of fixed size~$k$, yielding an $\mathcal{O}(k) = \mathcal{O}(1)$ permanent footprint per node regardless of network scale. During the initial broadcast, the joining node temporarily buffers up to $n$ acknowledgements, incurring an $\mathcal{O}(n)$ transient cost limited to the brief period before plan execution.

\subsection{Resistance to Fragmentation}
We analyze a null model: the probability that a simultaneous burst of $2k+2$ nodes joining an empty space partitions into two disconnected $(k+1)$-cliques. For such a split, every node in one group must fail to receive every broadcast from the other group. Let $p$ be the per-message loss probability. The splitting probability is:
$$P(\text{split}) = p^{2(k+1)^2}$$
This quantity decays exponentially with $k$, indicating that the protocol is highly resistant to fragmentation unless there is near-total communication failure.


\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\subsection{Concurrency and Vulnerability Model}
\label{subsec:concurrency_model}
While FINDING NEMO provides strong guarantees for sequential joins, its decentralized nature introduces a vulnerability when multiple nodes join simultaneously. Independent join plans may select overlapping targets; because each target has a hard degree limit, conflicting requests are denied, leaving rejected nodes deficient.

We formalize this contention using a thinned Poisson arrival process. Let $\lambda$ be the global arrival rate, $\tau$ the vulnerability window (delay from broadcast to execution), and $n$ the network size. A joining node selects $k/2$ targets uniformly from $n$ members. The localized request arrival rate at a specific target $A$ is $\lambda_A = \lambda \frac{k}{2n}$, and the number of requests $X$ during~$\tau$ follows:
$$P(X = x) = \frac{(\lambda_A \tau)^x e^{-\lambda_A \tau}}{x!}$$
A collision occurs when $X \ge 2$. The localized rate $\lambda_A$ is inversely proportional to~$n$: in small networks ($n \approx k$), even minor bursts saturate targets; in macro-scale overlays ($n \ge 10^5$), contention disperses across the population, driving $\lambda_A \to 0$. This analysis motivates subsequent self-healing mechanisms such as CHUALANH (omitted here for brevity).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:sim_setup}
We developed a discrete-event simulation framework to empirically validate the structural invariants, scalability, and concurrency boundaries of the protocol. The simulator models an asynchronous message-driven overlay where each joining node broadcasts an integration request, collects acknowledgements, formulates a join plan, and executes localized edge hand-offs. Network latency is modeled as an independent stochastic delay bounded by a vulnerability window~$\tau$.

Topology health is classified by three invariants:
(1)~\textbf{Non-mutual Edge:} a directional mismatch where $u$ lists $v$ but $u \notin N_v$;
(2)~\textbf{Problematic Node:} a node violating at least one invariant ($\deg(u) \neq k$, self-loops, duplicates, or non-mutual edges); and
(3)~\textbf{Network Convergence:} a terminal state where the overlay forms a single connected component with zero problematic nodes.
The primary metric is the \textit{Convergence Success Rate}~(\%), the proportion of independent trials achieving valid convergence. In total, 4{,}260 independent runs were executed.

\subsection{Test 1: Bootstrap Convergence in Sparse Networks}
\label{subsec:test1_bootstrap}
Test~1 evaluates Case~1, deploying $k+1$ nodes into an empty network simultaneously. We varied $k \in \{2, 4, 6, \dots, 20\}$ across 50 independent trials per degree (500 total runs).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{eval_fig/test1_bootstrap.eps}
\caption{Test 1: Bootstrap success rate vs.\ target degree $k \in [2, 20]$ in an empty network of $n = k+1$ nodes (50 trials per configuration, 500 total runs).}
\label{fig:test1_bootstrap}
\end{figure}

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:test1_bootstrap}, the protocol achieves 100\% convergence across every evaluated~$k$. During bootstrap, the node count does not exceed $k+1$; newly arrived nodes connect to all active members without eviction, forming a complete graph $K_{k+1}$ in which every node satisfies $\deg(u) = k$. These results confirm that bootstrap logic is unconditionally stable regardless of degree scale.

\subsection{Test 2: Scalability under Sequential Churn}
\label{subsec:test2_churn}
Test~2 evaluates Case~2 under prolonged sequential churn. Starting from a converged $k+1$ network, nodes are injected one by one up to $g$ additions, where $g \in \{5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 75, 100\}$. We swept $k \in \{4, 6, 8, 10\}$ with 20 trials per $(k, g)$ pair (640 total runs).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{eval_fig/test2_success_vs_g.eps}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{eval_fig/test2_bubble_k_vs_g.eps}
\caption{Test 2: (a)~Convergence success rate vs.\ sequential additions $g$ for $k \in \{4, 6, 8, 10\}$; (b)~parameter sweep bubble chart (20 trials per configuration, 640 total runs).}
\label{fig:test2_results}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:test2_results} shows that the protocol sustained 100\% convergence across all 32 configuration pairs. Even after $g = 100$ sequential joins (expanding the overlay to $100 + k + 1$ nodes), no problematic nodes or partitioned clusters emerged. Each join identified $k/2$ fully-connected targets, induced uniform evictions, and rewired displaced endpoints to the newcomer. This validates the Handshake Lemma conservation principle ($\Delta E = k/2$): sequential eviction hand-offs preserve exact $k$-regularity and global connectivity indefinitely.

\subsection{Test 3: Concurrency Boundaries in Minimal Overlays}
\label{subsec:test3_burst}
Test~3 stress-tests simultaneous arrivals by injecting a burst of $s$ nodes into an established $k+1$ network, with $s \in \{1, 2, \dots, 30\}$. We evaluated $k \in \{4, 6, 8, 10\}$ across 30 trials per configuration (1{,}560 total runs).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{eval_fig/test3_success_vs_s.eps}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{eval_fig/test3_bubble_k_vs_s.eps}
\caption{Test 3: (a)~Convergence success rate under simultaneous burst joins $s \in [1, 30]$ for $k \in \{4, 6, 8, 10\}$; (b)~parameter sweep bubble chart (30 trials per configuration, 1{,}560 total runs).}
\label{fig:test3_results}
\end{figure}

The results in Fig.~\ref{fig:test3_results} reveal a phase transition governed by degree conservation:
(1)~\textbf{Isolated joins ($s = 1$):} 100\% convergence across all~$k$.
(2)~\textbf{Pairwise bursts ($s = 2$):} convergence drops to 50--60\%, as two joining nodes sampling from a pool of only $k+1$ peers overlap roughly half the time.
(3)~\textbf{$s = 3$--$4$:} success plummets to 10--13\% and 3--7\%, respectively.
(4)~\textbf{$s \ge 5$:} the unassisted success rate reaches 0\% across all~$k$.

These observations validate the Poisson collision model of Section~\ref{subsec:concurrency_model}. In minimal networks ($n = k+1 \le 11$), target contention occurs with near certainty once $s \ge 5$, precisely delineating the unassisted concurrency boundary and confirming the necessity of auxiliary self-healing mechanisms in sparse overlays.

\subsection{Test 4: Concurrent Burst Resilience at Macro Scale}
\label{subsec:test4_macro}
To determine whether the concurrency bottleneck of Test~3 is inherent or merely a finite-size artifact, Test~4 replicates the same parameter sweep within an established overlay of $N = 100{,}000$ nodes (1{,}560 total runs).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{eval_fig/test4_success_vs_s.eps}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{eval_fig/test4_bubble_k_vs_s.eps}
\caption{Test 4: (a)~Convergence success rate under burst joins $s \in [1, 30]$ in a $N = 100{,}000$ overlay for $k \in \{4, 6, 8, 10\}$; (b)~parameter sweep bubble chart (30 trials per configuration, 1{,}560 total runs).}
\label{fig:test4_results}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:test4_results} reveals a striking contrast: across all 52 configurations, FINDING NEMO sustained 100\% convergence with zero problematic nodes, zero link asymmetries, and zero degree violations. The explanation follows directly from the scale-dependent arrival dynamics: each of the $s$ joining nodes selects $k/2$ targets from $N = 10^5$ members, yielding $M = s \cdot (k/2) \le 150$ total requests. The collision probability scales as $P(\text{collision}) \approx M^2 / (2N)$; for the most extreme burst ($s = 30, k = 10, M = 150$), this evaluates to approximately 11.25\%, while for moderate bursts ($s \le 10$), $P \le 1.25\%$. Combined with stochastic transmission delays that desynchronize commits, collisions are effectively eliminated. The unassisted protocol is therefore intrinsically robust at production scale; self-healing mechanisms are primarily required during low-density bootstrap or network partitions.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

% \subsubsection{Comparative Positioning.}
% FINDING NEMO occupies a specific niche in the overlay construction landscape. Unlike gossip-based protocols such as CYCLON~\cite{CYCLONInexpensiveMembership}, which maintain degree distributions that are only statistically symmetric ($\sigma^2 > 0$), FINDING NEMO enforces deterministic $k$-regularity ($\sigma^2 = 0$) at the point of entry. Unlike expander maintenance protocols~\cite{crucianiMaintainingBoundedDegree2025,angileriThresholdDrivenStreamingGraph2025} that rely on periodic edge refreshing or threshold-based reconnection to sustain expansion under continuous churn, our protocol concentrates its invariant enforcement at the atomic join event. The self-organizing approach of Zhao et~al.~\cite{PDFResilientNetworks} converges toward approximate $k$-regularity (allowing degrees in $[k, k+2]$) through iterated local edge swaps on an existing graph; in contrast, FINDING NEMO achieves exact regularity during incremental node arrival.

% These distinctions carry practical implications. PeerSwap~\cite{guerraouiPeerSwapPeerSamplerRandomness2024} provides provably uniform peer sampling on a fixed regular graph with polylogarithmic mixing time; it naturally complements FINDING NEMO, which constructs the very substrate that PeerSwap requires. The greedy peering analysis of Kiffer and Rajaraman~\cite{kifferStabilityP2PNetworks2024} demonstrates that performance-driven peer selection inevitably creates centralized hubs with disparate node performance. FINDING NEMO's uniform random target selection avoids this pathology by design. Conversely, the emergent multi-hub overlay of Legheraba et~al.~\cite{legherabaEmergentPeertoPeerMultiHub2026} deliberately produces hubs for low-diameter dissemination; this represents a complementary design philosophy suited to applications where broadcast speed outweighs load balance.

% \subsubsection{Security Considerations.}
% FINDING NEMO currently assumes benign participants. In adversarial environments, Byzantine nodes could manipulate join plans or selectively refuse evictions to degrade topology quality. Recent advances in Byzantine-tolerant peer sampling---Honeybee's verifiable random walks~\cite{zhangHoneybeeByzantineTolerant2025} and AUPE's collaborative debiasing with trusted enclaves~\cite{mukamAUPECollaborativeByzantine}---offer potential building blocks for hardening the protocol. Gupta and Pandurangan~\cite{guptaFullyDistributedProtocolConstructing2025} demonstrated that bounded-degree expander maintenance is feasible even under a full-information Byzantine adversary, suggesting that adversary-aware extensions of FINDING NEMO are a viable direction, though their design lies beyond the scope of this paper.

% \subsubsection{Evaluation Limitations.}
Our experimental evaluation relies on a discrete-event simulation framework rather than a deployment on real distributed infrastructure. The simulator models network latency as an independent, uniformly distributed random variable within a bounded vulnerability window $\tau \in [10, 50]$\,ms---a simplification that does not capture the tail-heavy latency distributions, packet loss, message reordering, and bandwidth heterogeneity characteristic of production networks. Furthermore, the simulator assumes reliable message delivery: no packets are dropped, no firewalls intervene, and no NAT traversal is required. All nodes possess identical computational capability, and the vulnerability window is constant across all nodes and across time.

The Test~4 macro-scale experiments ($N = 100{,}000$) employ a sampling-based initialization rather than simulating the full $100{,}000$-node network, verifying only that the joining nodes achieve correct degree integration against a representative subset of the overlay. As a result, global topology metrics such as spectral gap, diameter, and clustering coefficient of the full overlay are not measured. Evaluating these properties under realistic conditions---heterogeneous latency, partial message loss, node failures during join, and adversarial behavior---remains an important area for future work. A deployment-based evaluation on actual distributed infrastructure would complement our simulation study and strengthen confidence in the protocol's practical viability.


\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We presented FINDING NEMO, a decentralized join-and-evict protocol for constructing random regular overlays. By isolating the micro-level rules of node integration, we showed that exactly $k/2$ existing edges must be broken and rewired during each join to preserve bounded-degree properties---a direct consequence of the Handshake Lemma. We formalized the concurrent bootstrap race through a Poisson arrival model and empirically confirmed that while minimal networks require self-healing under bursts, macro-scale overlays ($N = 10^5$) achieve flawless convergence up to $s = 30$ simultaneous joins across 4{,}260 independent simulation runs.

Several directions merit further investigation. First, integrating a decentralized self-healing mechanism (such as CHUALANH) to repair deficient nodes caused by concurrent collisions in sparse topologies would extend the protocol's robustness envelope. Second, evaluating performance under heterogeneous latency distributions and partial message loss would bridge the gap between simulation and production deployment. Third, incorporating Byzantine-tolerant sampling primitives~\cite{zhangHoneybeeByzantineTolerant2025,mukamAUPECollaborativeByzantine} could harden the protocol against adversarial manipulation of join plans. Finally, measuring global spectral properties of overlays constructed by FINDING NEMO would establish formal expansion guarantees and connect our work to the broader expander-maintenance literature~\cite{crucianiMaintainingBoundedDegree2025,angileriThresholdDrivenStreamingGraph2025}.
